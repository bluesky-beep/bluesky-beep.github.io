Readings from 2024–present

During my doctoral studies I would often feel frustrated for not having enough time for non academic readings. Fortunately, the transition gap from student to working allowed me some time where I could prioritiz reading. Looking back in last two years, I’m amazed how certain books found me at exactly the right moment. 2024-2025 in particular was a deeply transformative phase of my life, and a handful of books I read during that time moved me in ways I didn’t expect.

The Fountainhead

— Ayn Rand

A friend’s recommendation. They insisted that, regardless of whether I agreed with its philosophy, it was worth experiencing. So it became one of the first reads after this long break. I must admit I binge read this book as I couldn’t stop myself. The book celebrates uncompromising individualism through Howard Roark, an architect who refuses to bend his principles for approval or success. I admired Roark’s commitment to excellence and authenticity. There is something inspiring about creating because you believe in the work itself rather than for applause. After having discussion with a friend of mine, he beautifully pointed out at times the philosophy feels too absolute. Human beings are rarely as independent as Rand imagines. As he continues we are shaped by communities, relationships, and shared responsibilities. I found myself appreciating the ambition while questioning the extremes.

Siddharta

— Herman Hesse

This one offered almost the opposite temperament. I read Siddhartha towards the end of my PhD, at a time when I was going through many transitions and carrying a fair amount of uncertainty about what comes next. This was also one of the rare books in my personal reading where I encountered Eastern philosophy through the eyes of a Western author. In the book, he moves through so many different circles of life—spiritual discipline, rebellion, desire, love, wealth, loss, and finally, a search for something beyond all of them and none of these experiences are presented as simply right or wrong. Each becomes part of what he understands. It is also one of the rare books where I encountered Eastern philosophy through a Western author’s eyes, and found myself wondering how much of the philosophy belonged to the traditions he was drawing from and how much belonged to Hesse’s own imagination.

The next two books pushed that question inward, and both were harder than expected and felt like intellectually dissectioning our tendencies and habit from different methods.

Karma

— Sadhguru

I bought it while travelling in India with my mother during New year. The central idea of the book is that karma is less about fate or a cosmic system of rewards and punishments more about accumulated tendencies and unconscious patterns and changes. Rather than blaming circumstances, the book encourages one to look inward. As the author himself has repeatdly said “The only way out In”. The book attempts to reclaim the original meaning of karma. In everyday conversation, we often reduce it to “good karma” or “bad karma” which is a shorthand for actions and their consequences on contrary the book explores karma as the very mechanism through which our identities and habits are formed. Some concepts rely heavily on spiritual intuition and hard to logically argue, which may not resonate with every reader. Still, I found many ideas surprisingly practical.

Freedom from the Known

— J. Krishnamurti

I started reading this one while travelling through Shanghai. It quickly became one of the most intellectually demanding books I have ever attempted. I often read and reread passages to fully understand it and i still doubt if I have fully grasp it. The book does heavily alters your view on life and everything that drives a life. Krishnamurti challenges nearly every assumption we carry about identity, belief, authority, and thought itself. He isn’t interested in giving answers instead, he dismantles the questions itself. The ideas are subtle, and I often felt I understood each sentence individually while missing the larger picture. I am very much confident that I have only understood perhaps 10/% of it. I still haven’t finish the book as it is very strong dose and hence would hesitate recommended anyone so far unless they are seekers.

Stories of Sahara

— Sanmao

I reread this before and after reading the above two books and was a nice shift in reading as it wasn’t strenous. This was a gift, which somehow made reading it feel more intimate, I was told I would resonate a lot with author and I did enjoyed Sanmao’s wild and joyfull outlook on life. Sanmao writes with remarkable honesty about her life in the Sahara. The stories feel like both travel writing and conversations with someone who notices beauty in ordinary moments. The warmth, humour, and vulnerability of Sanmao made me feel as if I were travelling alongside her, it makes you realize similarity and common struggle of people all over the world. As a women, it also makes you wonder that even in this modern era the freedom and rights for women are so uneven across the world. The emotional sincerity makes the book unforgettable.

White Nights

— Fyodor Dostoevsky

Read this around February 2026 as I had wanted to read Dostoevsky for years. Instead of beginning with one of his heavy long novels, I thought White Nights would be a gentle introduction. It is astonishing how much loneliness and hope Dostoevsky captures in such a short work. The story reminded me how easily people can build entire worlds from brief encounters and impossible expectations. The emotional intensity can feel overwhelming, but perhaps that is exactly what makes it memorable. It certainly convinced me to continue exploring Dostoevsky.

Desire

— Haruki Murakami

Even though I have read other Murakami (too famous) books but this one I randomly discovered while browsing a bookstore during a layover at Seoul Airport. This book is very short that I was able to finish it between my flight from Seoul to Tokyo. It is a collection of small intriguing stories with open end interpretations. Murakami is master of making ordinary moments quietly mysterious. All the stories although indepent revolves around common thread of one inexplainable yet deeply transformative moments. Like much of Murakami’s work, ambiguity is both its strength and its weakness. Some questions remain unanswered, which can either feel profound or frustrating depending on the day.

Gora

— Rabindranath Tagore

I had long admired Tagore’s poetry and wanted to experience his longer fiction. Gora explores identity, nationalism, religion, and belonging with remarkable nuance. What impressed me most was that Tagore rarely presents easy heroes or villains. Instead, he invites the reader to question inherited beliefs. (Reading is still in progress). Will update on this when finished.

Two of these books are unfinished, and one I may never finish. That could feel like an honest place to end. The books that found me in these two years did not resolve much; they mostly replaced my questions with better ones, which is, I think, what I was actually looking for.

With the help of some dear friends we have started a book club this year where we have been discussing to some old classsic books to albums. Currently, the plan to expand this book club to more people is under the process.
