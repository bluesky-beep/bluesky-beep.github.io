During my doctoral studies I would often feel frustrated for not having enough time to read. Fortunately, the transition time from student to working allowed me some time where I could prioritiz reading. Looking back at 2025 and 2026, I'm amazed how certain books found me at exactly the right moment. 2024-2025 in particular was a deeply transformative phase of my life, and a handful of books I read during that time moved me in ways I didn't expect.  

---

## The Fountainhead — Ayn Rand

A close friend recommended it to me. They insisted that, regardless of whether I agreed with its philosophy, it was worth experiencing.
The book celebrates uncompromising individualism through Howard Roark, an architect who refuses to bend his principles for approval or success.
I admired Roark's commitment to excellence and authenticity. There is something inspiring about creating because you believe in the work itself rather than for applause. After having discussion with a friend of mine, he beautifully pointed out at times the philosophy feels too absolute. Human beings are rarely as independent as Rand imagines. As he continues we are shaped by communities, relationships, and shared responsibilities.
I found myself appreciating the ambition while questioning the extremes.

---

## Stories of Sahara — Sanmao

This was a gift from a friend, which somehow made reading it feel more intimate, I was told I would resonate a lot and I did enjoyed Sanmao's wild and joyfull personality.
Sanmao writes with remarkable honesty about her life in the Sahara. The stories feel less like  both travel writing and conversations with someone who notices beauty in ordinary moments.
The warmth, humour, and vulnerability of Sanmao made me feel as if I were travelling alongside her, it also makes you realize similarity and common struggle of people all over the world. As a women, it also makes you wonder that even in this modern era the freedom and rights for women are so uneven across the world. The emotional sincerity makes the book unforgettable.

---

## Desire — Haruki Murakami
Even though I have read other Murakami (too famous)  books but this one I randomly discovered while browsing a bookstore during a layover at Seoul Airport. This book is very short that I was able to finish it between my flight from Seoul to Tokyo. 
It is a collection of small intriguing stories with open end interpretations. Murakami is master of making ordinary moments quietly mysterious.
All the stories although indepent revolves around common thread of one inexplainable yet deeply transformative moments. Like much of Murakami's work, ambiguity is both its strength and its weakness. Some questions remain unanswered, which can either feel profound or frustrating depending on the day.

---

## Karma — Sadhguru
I bought it while travelling in India with my mother last year.
The central idea—that karma is less about fate or a cosmic system of rewards and punishments more about accumulated tendencies and unconscious patterns—changes. Rather than blaming circumstances, the book encourages one to look inward. As the author himself has repeatdly said "The only way out In". The book attempts to reclaim the original meaning of *karma*. In everyday conversation, we often reduce it to "good karma" or "bad karma"—a shorthand for actions and their consequences on contrary the book explores karma as the very mechanism through which our identities and habits are formed. Some concepts rely heavily on spiritual intuition and hard to logically argue, which may not resonate with every reader. Still, I found many ideas surprisingly practical.

---

## White Nights — Fyodor Dostoevsky

Read this around February 2026 as I had wanted to read Dostoevsky for years.

Instead of beginning with one of his heavy long novels, I thought *White Nights* would be a gentle introduction. It is astonishing how much loneliness and hope Dostoevsky captures in such a short work. The story reminded me how easily people can build entire worlds from brief encounters and impossible expectations. The emotional intensity can feel overwhelming, but perhaps that is exactly what makes it memorable.
It certainly convinced me to continue exploring Dostoevsky.

---

## Freedom from the Known — J. Krishnamurti
I started reading this one while travelling through Shanghai. It quickly became one of the most intellectually demanding books I had ever attempted. I often read and reread passages to fully understand it and i still doubt if I have fully grasp it.  The book does permanently alters your view on life and everything that drives a life. Krishnamurti challenges nearly every assumption we carry about identity, belief, authority, and thought itself. He isn't interested in giving answers. Instead, he dismantles the questions. The ideas are subtle, and I often felt I understood each sentence individually while missing the larger picture. I am very much confident that I have only understood perhaps 10/% of it.
I still haven't finish the book as it is very strong dose and hence would also not have recommended anyone so far.
